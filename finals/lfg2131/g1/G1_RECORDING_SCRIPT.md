The script below follows the 11-slide revised deck. First-person wording is a proposed draft for Laszlo to check against what he genuinely decided and explored. It does not present future work or planned evaluations as completed evidence.

### Slide 1 — Title (0:00–0:18)

“Hi, I'm Laszlo Godde. TOUCHLINE: Tactical Duel lets you manage a club through six fixtures, read match evidence and face a coach that adapts. I want the tension of a fair tactical chess match.”

### Slide 2 — Player and motivation (0:18–0:40)

“I'm a Football Manager fan, designing for players motivated by mastery. If this works, I should observe players study a defeat, name the opposing pattern and deliberately change tactics without my help.”

### Slide 3 — Core loop (0:40–1:03)

“The player scouts public reports, chooses the XI and tactics, then sees chances, pressure and fatigue. At halftime they interpret this evidence and make one adjustment. After the result, they prepare the next fixture. Six matches form one complete episode.”

### Slide 4 — Concepts and pillars (1:03–1:30)

“I explored a full modern career simulator, but its enormous database and systems exceeded the semester. My Classic midterm tested a compact 1982-style remake. Tactical Duel won because it preserves that scope while testing a new rivalry. My pillars are readable cues, fair counters and learning from losses.”

### Slide 5 — References and rights (1:30–1:57)

“Kevin Toms' 1982 Football Manager inspired compact squad selection and match results. Modern Football Manager inspired tactical adjustments. I change those ideas by making a bounded rival central. The clubs and illustrations are fictional and original. I borrow no licensed screenshots, database, logos or code. My Classic PR supplies the student-built foundation.”

### Slide 6 — System and information boundary (1:57–2:25)

“The engine owns the private state: hidden selections and simulation seed. A filter shares only two public reports, revealed half events and legal actions. The rival proposes one tactical counter. The engine validates or rejects it, then shows public feedback. The agent never sees hidden decisions or changes rules directly.”

### Slide 7 — Agent hypothesis and baseline (2:25–2:53)

“The scripted comparator uses the same three legal tactics but follows fixed rules. The agent can use public history. Without that memory, I expect players to lose the feeling of being studied. I will observe whether an uncoached player identifies the rival's approach and responds deliberately.”

### Slide 8 — Scope and cuts (2:53–3:17)

“Classic already has the six-match simulation, squad fatigue, standings and replay tests. The adaptive rival and genuine player observation remain unproven. I will prototype one encounter, then retain six fixtures and halftime decisions. I'll cut animation and scouting embellishments first. Licensed content, multiplayer and 3D stay outside scope.”

### Slide 9 — Numbers and basis (3:17–3:46)

“The budget allows two calls per fixture, twelve per session. With 2,000 input and 300 output tokens each, Google's published Gemini Flash-Lite rates imply about $0.00384 per session. The ceiling is ten cents. I target p95 below three seconds, with a five-second timeout and scripted fallback. These remain unmeasured projections.”

### Slide 10 — Risk profile (3:46–4:15)

“IP, audience and team coordination are Medium risks; technology, mechanics and timeline are High. Their shared cause is spending time on adaptation players cannot perceive. A strict action schema, one-match prototype and uncoached test mitigate that. Agents propose engineering fixes; I approve creative decisions and scope.”

### Slide 11 — Decision (4:15–4:38)

“I ask G1 to approve one complete encounter testing the adaptive coach against a fixed rival, with feedback and fallback. Hold if information boundaries, tactical readability or budget and scope remain unclear. The instructor decides; I address corrections. My Braintrust question is: would you notice and counter this adaptation? Thank you.”
